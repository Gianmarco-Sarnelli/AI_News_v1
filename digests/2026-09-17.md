### How Model Growth, Recursion, and Boundary Operators Influence Scaling Exponents
*emergent_mind · [https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2609.19107](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2609.19107)*

This paper pushes back on the common view that scaling laws are largely architecture-agnostic, showing that certain structural choices—specifically recursive/looped transformer depth—can shift the scaling exponent itself, not just the constant factor. That distinction matters enormously: a better constant gives you a one-time win, but a better exponent means the efficiency gap widens as compute grows, i.e., exponential rather than linear improvement. The headline result—a 7.4B parameter model with growth architecture matching GPT-3 13B on the CORE benchmark using ~20x less compute—is a striking efficiency claim if it holds up under independent replication. Looped/recursive-depth transformers reuse weights across multiple passes, effectively trading parameter count for compute depth, which resonates with other 'weight-sharing' and 'universal transformer' lines of work but here framed explicitly through the scaling-law lens. The paper also examines 'boundary operators,' suggesting a broader taxonomy of architectural interventions beyond just recursion. Open questions: does this exponent shift hold at larger scales (100B+) or is it an artifact of the specific benchmark and model family tested? Also unclear is inference-time cost—looping architectures often trade training compute for inference latency, so the practical efficiency win could be partially illusory depending on deployment context. Still, if scaling exponents are genuinely modifiable by architecture, it reopens a research direction (beyond just data/compute scaling) that had been somewhat sidelined since Chinchilla-style scaling laws became dogma.

### Infinite-Parameter LLMs: Generating and Adapting Weights from Live Data
*emergent_mind · [https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2609.18842](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2609.18842)*

This work targets a real deployment gap: MoE models achieve huge capacity by activating sparse expert subsets per token, but all of that capacity is frozen at deploy time—user-supplied facts, corrections, and session-specific knowledge must be crammed into the context window and re-processed every turn rather than being 'learned.' The proposed approach generates or adapts weights dynamically from live interaction data, effectively blurring the line between in-context learning and parameter updates. This connects to several active threads: hypernetworks (generating weights from inputs), fast weights/LoRA-style adapters applied at inference time, and retrieval-augmented generation as an alternative solution to the same problem. The appeal is that dynamically generated weights could, in principle, be more sample-efficient and persistent than prompt-based context, potentially solving long-context degradation and repeated re-reading costs. The main caveats: weight-generation-on-the-fly raises serious concerns about stability, safety (dynamically mutable weights are harder to audit/red-team than static ones), and computational overhead of generating/adapting parameters per session. It's also unclear how this compares empirically against simpler alternatives like continual fine-tuning, LoRA hot-swapping, or advanced RAG—the summary doesn't specify a rigorous head-to-head. Still, the framing—that current 'infinite-parameter' MoE scaling still leaves live data as an untapped resource—is a good articulation of a real practical bottleneck for deployed LLM products, and hypernetwork-style solutions are a plausible research direction worth tracking regardless of whether this specific method wins out.

### Google DeepMind starts an AGI think tank
*rundown_ai · [https://institute.deepmind.com/essays/introducing-the-deepmind-institute/?utm_source=therundownai.beehiiv.com&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=rundown-0918](https://institute.deepmind.com/essays/introducing-the-deepmind-institute/?utm_source=therundownai.beehiiv.com&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=rundown-0918)*

Google DeepMind's creation of an internal AGI-focused think tank, helmed by Hassabis, Shane Legg (DeepMind's co-founder and self-titled 'Chief AGI Scientist'), and James Manyika, is notable less for technical content and more as an institutional/political signal. The essays reportedly claim AGI is close but 'lacks consistency and creativity,' with an expectation these gaps close 'soon'—a bold, unhedged forecast from people at the center of frontier model development, though each essay carries a disclaimer it isn't official Google policy (a hedge that lets DeepMind float ideas without corporate commitment). Topics like detecting deception in AI reasoning and preparing labor markets for disruption mirror concerns Anthropic and OpenAI have also been vocalizing, suggesting a convergence among major labs toward more public AGI-risk discourse—happening in the same news cycle as Zuckerberg publicly rejecting Anthropic's 'coordinated slowdown' framing. This creates an interesting three-way tension: Anthropic pushing caution, Meta pushing competitive urgency with self-regulation, and DeepMind attempting a more academic, 'let's think together about the transition' posture. Skeptically, think-tank essays from a company with massive commercial incentive to appear both safety-conscious and imminent-AGI-capable should be read as strategic communication as much as genuine forecasting; the 'AGI is near' framing itself is contestable and has been walked back before industry-wide. Worth watching for how concretely these essays translate into actual research agendas or policy engagement versus remaining a PR/thought-leadership exercise.
